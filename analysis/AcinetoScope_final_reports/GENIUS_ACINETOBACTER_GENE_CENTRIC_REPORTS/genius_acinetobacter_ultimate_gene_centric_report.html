AI Assistant Guide – Unleash the Power of AI for Genomic Epidemiology

This guide shows you how to leverage large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini to interact with your A. baumannii dataset – turning static reports into dynamic conversations.

Why Use AI for Genomic Data Analysis?

Modern AI models excel at:

  • Pattern recognition – spotting epidemiological trends, clone associations, and co‑occurrence networks.
  • Natural language queries – ask in plain English, get instant answers without writing code.
  • Hypothesis generation – uncover unexpected correlations that merit experimental follow‑up.
  • Literature synthesis – connect your findings with published resistance mechanisms and clinical guidelines.

Scientific note: AI is pattern‑finding, not causal‑inferring. Use it to suggest, then verify with wet‑lab or clinical correlation.

How to Feed This Report to AI

You have three powerful options:

  1. Upload the JSON file – genius_acinetobacter_ultimate_gene_centric_report.json contains all structured data. Upload it to ChatGPT (Advanced Data Analysis), Claude, or Gemini. Best for precise quantitative queries.
  2. Upload the HTML report – Modern AI tools can read HTML and extract tables. Upload the .html file directly – the AI will parse the tables and text. Great for visual context.
  3. Copy‑paste specific tables – If you only need a quick insight, copy a table (e.g., AMR gene list) and paste it into the chat. Instant, no file upload needed.

Pro tip: For best results, tell the AI: "You are a bioinformatician analysing A. baumannii genomes. The attached data contains typing, AMR, virulence, BACMET, and mutation information. Answer my questions with references to the data."

Scientifically Relevant Questions to Ask

🧬 Epidemiology & Clonality
  • What are the most common Pasteur STs in this dataset?
  • Which capsule types (K:O) are dominant?
  • Are there any ST‑capsule combinations with >2 isolates?
  • Which clones carry the most resistance genes?
💊 Antimicrobial Resistance
  • How many samples carry OXA-23? What are their STs?
  • Are there any NDM‑positive samples? What is their capsule type?
  • Which AMR genes co‑occur most frequently?
  • What is the distribution of colistin resistance genes (mcr, pmr)?
🦠 Virulence & Biofilm
  • Which samples carry biofilm genes (ompA, csu)?
  • List all isolates with the T6SS (tss, hcp).
  • Is there a correlation between capsule type and virulence gene carriage?
🧪 Mutations & Biocides
  • What are the most frequent point mutations in gyrA or parC?
  • Are there any 23S rRNA mutations (tigecycline resistance)?
  • Which samples carry qac genes (disinfectant resistance)?

Beyond tables: Ask the AI to “write a summary of the resistance profile for ST2” or “compare virulence gene carriage between K1 and K2 capsule types” – the report contains all data.

Scientific Rigour & Ethical AI Use

  • AI is your co‑pilot, not the pilot. Always interpret AI‑generated insights in the context of your local epidemiology, clinical guidelines, and laboratory validation.
  • Verify, verify, verify. Cross‑check critical calls (e.g., carbapenemase presence, colistin resistance) with primary literature, genome browsers, or secondary tools.
  • No patient‑identifiable data. Only upload aggregated, de‑identified genomic data. This report contains no clinical metadata.
  • Transparency in publications. If you use AI for exploratory analysis, mention it in the methods (e.g., “AI‑assisted pattern discovery was performed using a large language model, followed by manual curation”).
  • AI hallucination is real. If the AI confidently tells you that blaOXA-23 is found in E. coli or that tigecycline resistance is caused by a mutation in rpoB – don’t believe it. Treat every AI statement as a hypothesis, not a fact.

A (Mostly Serious) AI Survival Guide

  • If the AI says “I don’t know” – trust it. It’s being honest.
  • If the AI says “It is widely known” – ask for a reference. It may have made it up.
  • If the AI offers a completely novel evolutionary theory – check if your coffee is spiked.
  • Remember: AI won’t take your job – but a microbiologist who knows how to use AI might! So learn it, use it, and always keep a healthy dose of scepticism. 😉

Final thought: The best AI‑human partnership is one where the AI does the heavy pattern‑lifting, and you do the heavy thinking. Happy (and careful) exploring!
